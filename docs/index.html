Estimating flood inundation probability in space

Every 500-meter cell in Calgary receives a prediction interpreted as the probability that the area will be inundated by flood water. The model is trained and validated on Calgary, then transferred to two US cities chosen along a terrain gradient. The transfer measures what the model has learned and where its validity ends.

Calgary is tiled with a 500 m fishnet. A cell receives a positive label when more than five percent of its area falls within the City of Calgary’s official 1 % annual-chance flood extent — a modeled steady-state hazard rather than a specific storm.

Positive cells trace the Bow and Elbow river valleys and the Nose Creek corridor. Elsewhere the label is zero, and it is zero by a wide margin: only — of cells are positive. That class imbalance drives every downstream decision about thresholds and error costs.

Source: City of Calgary Open Data, 1:100 flood inundation extent (2019 revision). Cells with intersection ≥ 5 % of area coded positive.

Euclidean distance from each cell centroid to the nearest mapped river polyline. The gradient is sharp: flooded cells cluster within a few hundred meters of a channel, and the density collapses beyond roughly one kilometer.

This is the single strongest predictor in the specification. Darker cells sit closer to a channel; the ridge of dark cells traces the Bow–Elbow confluence in the city core.

Mean terrain slope in degrees from a satellite elevation model. Contrary to intuition, slope runs slightly higher in flooded cells — not because slope causes flooding, but because channels sit at the base of valley walls, so the flood surface brushes against steep terrain.

Slope alone marks valley topography rather than hydrologic risk. Elevation is what separates the valley floor from the valley wall.

Mean cell elevation, meters above sea level. Water accumulates at low elevation; elevation and slope together distinguish the valley floor, which floods, from the valley wall, which does not.

Elevation without slope would misclassify entire plateaus. Slope without elevation would misclassify hillsides. The two features carry complementary signal that a linear model combines cleanly.

Share of the cell covered by pavement and rooftops, from the city landcover composite. Impervious surfaces convert rainfall into runoff instead of infiltration.

Alone this is a weak signal — downtown Calgary is largely impervious and does not flood. Combined with proximity to a channel, it becomes a meaningful risk amplifier along the river corridor.

The four features are z-scored within each city before fitting, so every coefficient reads as the change in log-odds per standard deviation. Standardization serves twice: coefficients become directly comparable within the model, and different instruments measuring the same quantity land on the same scale during transfer.

Signs are stable across bootstrap draws. Distance to river (β ≈ −2.14) and elevation (β ≈ −1.32) lower the odds. Impervious share (β ≈ +0.61) raises them. Slope alone is negligible (β ≈ −0.28) once elevation is present. The map shows the fitted probability surface — high near the corridor, near zero elsewhere.

Fit with caret::train(method = "glm", family = "binomial") on a 70/30 training split with a fixed seed.

Only nine percent of cells flood. A conventional 0.5 cutoff would classify almost everything as negative and bury the minority class. The decision threshold is instead tuned with Youden’s J statistic (sensitivity + specificity − 1), which lands near 0.10.

The map shows the fitted probability surface binarized at that threshold: dark cells exceed it, mid cells sit within one-third of it, light cells fall below. The asymmetric cost is deliberate — a missed flood cell propagates into planning decisions with real consequences; a spurious flag is cheap to review. Erring toward recall is the correct side to err on.

Neighboring 500 m cells share almost all of their features, so a random 70/30 split leaks spatial information across the boundary and inflates every performance number. The honest protocol trains on one half of the city and evaluates the other. Here the model is refit on the western half of Calgary and predicted on the eastern half.

Discrimination survives the spatial test almost untouched — sensitivity holds near 0.99 and AUC near 0.95. Calibration does not: specificity drops from 0.87 under the random split to 0.66 under the spatial holdout. The eastern half is systematically over-predicted.

The ranking survives; the absolute probability does not. Transfers to Denver and Minneapolis are therefore reported as relative risk rather than literal probability.

Predicted probabilities binned by quintile. The vast majority of cells sit close to zero, and risk concentrates along the river network. In Calgary, flood hazard is not spread across a gradient — it is confined to identifiable corridors.

Spatial-holdout sensitivity —, AUC —, balanced accuracy —. Strong ranking in absolute terms and unusually strong for a four-feature specification.

Predictions classified at the tuned threshold. False positives adjoin true positives along the same corridors — misclassification concentrates at the floodplain edge rather than being scattered randomly. False negatives are rare and isolated.

At a nine percent base rate, a flagged cell with moderate precision still carries a fourfold concentration of risk relative to the average cell. The map should be read as a ranking tool, not a verdict.

Semi-arid, continental, at the Rocky Mountain foothills, drained by the South Platte and Cherry Creek. The flood mechanism matches Calgary’s — heavy rain on steep terrain drains into valleys where the city sits, and the impervious core amplifies runoff.

Predicted mass sits near zero, the upper tail is bounded, and high relative risk follows the Platte corridor and the northwest lowlands. The distribution shape matches Calgary — the first-order transferability result.

Riverine and continental like Calgary, but the terrain is flat prairie along the Mississippi. With elevation and slope variation stripped away, distance to river is the only feature with meaningful spread to work with.

Predictions saturate in a narrow band along the river trench and the Chain of Lakes. The model reduces to a river buffer — and that reduction is itself the finding: this specification encodes foothills-valley hydrology, not a universal flood signature.

Overlaying the three predicted-probability densities compresses the transfer story into a single chart. Calgary and Denver share a shape: a spike near zero with a bounded upper tail. Minneapolis grows a saturated tail that neither of the others has.

Two cities with terrain, one distributional family. One city without terrain, distributional divergence. The boundary of validity is visible in this shape difference.

Method, in one sentence

The model predicts flood inundation well where terrain drives hydrology, transfers to a terrain-similar city with its risk ranking intact, and degrades to a river buffer on flat ground. That boundary defines where this class of model can support planning decisions — and where it cannot.
