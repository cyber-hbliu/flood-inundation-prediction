A spatial model, its transfer, and its limits

Where the water goes.

A logistic model estimates, for every 500-meter cell of Calgary, the probability that it lies inside the 1-in-100-year flood extent. Trained on foothills hydrology, the model is then carried to two American cities to find out what it actually learned—and where it stops working.

Calgary, Alberta
DataCalgary

The label is the 1-in-100-year extent

Calgary is divided into a fishnet of 500-meter cells. A cell is labeled inundated when more than five percent of its area falls inside the city’s official 1% annual-chance flood extent, a modeled steady-state hazard rather than a single storm.

Feature 1 of 4Calgary

Distance to river

Each cell’s centroid distance to the river network. Water floods what it can reach, so proximity is the first and strongest candidate predictor. Darker cells sit closer to a channel.

Feature 2 of 4Calgary

Slope

Mean terrain slope from a satellite elevation model. Slope runs slightly higher in flooded cells because channels sit below valley walls—it partly marks valley terrain rather than causing flooding. The next feature separates the two.

Feature 3 of 4Calgary

Elevation

Water pools where the land is low. Elevation and slope together distinguish valley floors, which flood, from valley walls, which merely stand next to flooding.

Feature 4 of 4Calgary

Impervious share

The share of each cell covered by pavement and rooftops, from the city’s landcover composite. Impervious ground sheds water instead of absorbing it, pushing runoff toward wherever the terrain sends it.

ModelCalgary

Four features, one logistic regression

Features are z-scored within the city, so every coefficient reads as the change in flood odds per standard deviation, and the coefficients are directly comparable. Distance and elevation push odds down; impervious share pushes them up.

Standardization is also what makes the later city transfer defensible: each city’s features are measured relative to that city, so different instruments measuring the same quantity land on the same scale.

70/30 train–test split, seed fixed. Full specification in the methods report.

ThresholdCalgary

0.5 would be the wrong cutoff

Only nine percent of cells flood, so a conventional 0.5 cutoff buries the minority class. The threshold is instead tuned with Youden’s index, landing near 0.10. In flood planning a missed flood cell costs far more than a spurious flag, so this is the right side to err on.

ValidationCalgary

The honest test is spatial

Neighboring cells are nearly identical, so a random split leaks spatial information and flatters the model. Training on the west half and predicting the east is the honest estimate of performance in unseen territory.

Discrimination survives the spatial test almost untouched. Calibration does not—the east is over-flagged—so transfers are presented as relative risk, not literal probability.

ResultsCalgary

Where the model is wrong

False positives ring the true positives along the same corridors—near misses at the floodplain edge, not noise. False negatives are rare and isolated. At a nine percent base rate, a flagged cell carries a fourfold concentration of risk even at moderate precision.

Transfer 1 of 2Denver

Denver, the analog

Semi-arid, continental, at the Rocky Mountain foothills, on the South Platte and Cherry Creek. Same flood mechanism as Calgary: heavy rain on steep terrain draining into valleys where the city sits.

The transfer behaves like a sibling. Predicted mass sits near zero, the tail is bounded, and high relative risk traces the Platte corridor and the northwest lowlands.

Transfer 2 of 2Minneapolis

Minneapolis, the contrast

Riverine and continental like Calgary, but flat prairie along the Mississippi. With terrain variation gone, the model has only distance to lean on—and it shows. Predictions saturate in a hard band hugging the river trench and the Chain of Lakes.

The model has degenerated into a river buffer. That failure is the point: it reveals that what the model learned is foothills-valley hydrology, not a universal flood signature.

Pipeline

Method, in one sentence

Open data in, R pipeline through, honest validation out: a model that predicts flood risk well where terrain drives hydrology, transfers cleanly to a terrain-similar city, and visibly degrades on flat ground—and knowing that boundary is what makes it usable.
