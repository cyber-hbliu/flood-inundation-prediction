Spatial statistical modeling · logistic regression · transferability

Estimating flood inundation probability in space

Every 500-meter cell in Calgary receives a prediction interpreted as the probability that the area will be inundated by flood water. The model is trained and validated on Calgary, then transferred to two US cities chosen along a terrain gradient. The transfer measures what the model has actually learned and where its validity ends.

DataCalgary

The label is the 1-in-100-year flood extent

Calgary is tiled with a 500 m fishnet. A cell receives a positive label when more than five percent of its area falls inside the City of Calgary’s official 1 % annual-chance flood extent — a modeled steady-state hazard rather than a specific storm event.

Inundated cells trace the Bow and Elbow river valleys and the Nose Creek corridor. Elsewhere the label is zero, and it is zero everywhere by a wide margin: only — of cells are labelled positive, which shapes every downstream decision about thresholds and error costs.

Source: City of Calgary Open Data, 1:100 flood inundation extent, 2019 revision. Any cell whose polygon intersection exceeds 5 % of the fishnet cell area is coded positive.

Feature 1 of 4Calgary

Distance to river

Euclidean distance from each cell centroid to the nearest mapped river polyline. The gradient is sharp: flooded cells cluster within a few hundred meters of a channel, and the density collapses beyond roughly one kilometer.

This makes distance the single strongest predictor in the specification. Darker cells in the map are closer to a channel; the ridge of dark cells follows the Bow-Elbow confluence in the city core.

Feature 2 of 4Calgary

Slope

Mean terrain slope in degrees, computed from a satellite-derived elevation model. Contrary to intuition, slope runs slightly higher in flooded cells — not because slope causes flooding, but because channels sit at the base of valley walls, so the flood surface itself brushes against steep terrain.

Slope on its own is therefore a marker of valley topography rather than a hydrologic driver. The next feature is what separates the valley floor from the valley wall.

Feature 3 of 4Calgary

Elevation

Mean cell elevation, meters above sea level. Water accumulates at low elevation; elevation and slope together distinguish the valley floor, which floods, from the valley wall, which does not.

Reading elevation without slope would misclassify entire plateaus. Reading slope without elevation would misclassify hillsides. The two features carry complementary signal that a linear model can combine cleanly.

Feature 4 of 4Calgary

Impervious share

Share of the cell covered by pavement and rooftops, derived from the city’s landcover composite. Impervious surfaces convert rainfall to runoff instead of infiltration, which raises the flood odds wherever terrain concentrates the flow.

Alone this is a weak signal — downtown is largely impervious and does not flood. Combined with proximity to a channel it becomes a meaningful risk amplifier along the river corridor.

ModelSpecification

Four features, one logistic regression

The four features are z-scored within each city before fitting, so every coefficient reads as the change in log-odds per standard deviation. Standardization matters twice: it makes the coefficients directly comparable, and it makes different instruments measuring the same quantity land on the same scale during transfer.

The signs are stable across bootstrap draws. Distance and elevation lower the odds; impervious share raises them; slope is negligible on its own once elevation is present.

Fit with caret::train(method = "glm", family = "binomial") on a 70/30 training split with a fixed seed. Full specification in the methods report.

ThresholdClass imbalance

Threshold selection under class imbalance

Only nine percent of cells flood, so a conventional 0.5 cutoff would bury the minority class. The decision threshold is instead tuned with Youden’s J statistic, which maximizes sensitivity plus specificity minus one, and lands near 0.10.

The choice reflects an asymmetric cost. A missed flood cell propagates into planning decisions with real consequences; a spurious flag is cheap to review. Erring toward recall is the correct side to err on for this class of decision.

ValidationSpatial holdout

The honest test is spatial

Neighboring cells share almost all of their features, so a random 70/30 split leaks spatial information across the boundary and inflates every performance number. A more realistic protocol trains on one half of the city and predicts the other.

Here the model is refit on the western half of Calgary and evaluated on the eastern half. This estimates the performance the model would deliver on genuinely unseen territory, which is the setting for any city-to-city transfer.

ValidationRandom vs. spatial

Discrimination survives, calibration does not

Ranking metrics — sensitivity and AUC — barely move when the split becomes spatial. The model still discriminates flooded from non-flooded cells almost as well on new terrain.

Specificity drops noticeably. Under the spatial holdout the eastern half is systematically over-predicted: the model flags more cells than it should. This is a calibration failure, not a ranking failure, and it is the reason results in Denver and Minneapolis are presented as relative risk rather than literal probability.

ResultsError geography

Where the model errs

The map recolors by outcome at the tuned threshold. False positives adjoin the true positives along the same corridors, so misclassification concentrates at the floodplain edge rather than being scattered randomly. False negatives are rare and isolated.

At a nine percent base rate, even a flagged cell with moderate precision carries a fourfold concentration of risk relative to the average cell. The map should be read as a ranking tool, not a verdict.

Transfer 1 of 2Denver, CO

Denver, the terrain analog

Semi-arid, continental, at the Rocky Mountain foothills, drained by the South Platte and Cherry Creek. The flood mechanism matches Calgary: heavy rain on steep terrain drains into valleys where the city sits, and the impervious core amplifies the runoff.

Predicted mass sits near zero, the upper tail is bounded, and high relative risk follows the Platte corridor and the northwest lowlands. The distribution shape matches Calgary, which is the first-order transferability result.

Transfer 2 of 2Minneapolis, MN

Minneapolis, the flat contrast

Riverine and continental like Calgary, but the terrain is flat prairie along the Mississippi. With elevation and slope variation stripped away, distance to river is the only feature with meaningful spread left to work with.

Predictions saturate in a narrow band along the river trench and around the Chain of Lakes. The model degrades to a river buffer — and that reduction is itself the finding: this specification encodes foothills-valley hydrology, not a universal flood signature.

EquityPoverty overlay

Flood risk and poverty

Predicted risk is joined to tract-level poverty rates from the 2023 American Community Survey. In Denver the association is positive and statistically significant (—, p = 0.003): higher-poverty tracts carry higher predicted risk, consistent with the documented geography of the South Platte corridor.

In Minneapolis the correlation is essentially zero (—, p = 0.56). The null is a statement about this model on flat terrain, not a finding that Minneapolis has no flood-equity problem — the model there simply cannot distinguish risk levels finely enough to test the relationship.

Method, in one sentence

The model predicts flood inundation well where terrain drives hydrology, transfers to a terrain-similar city with its risk ranking intact, and degrades to a river buffer on flat ground. That boundary defines where this class of model can support planning decisions — and where it cannot.
